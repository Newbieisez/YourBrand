Build Your Visual Brand

Turn the choices you have made into a simple visual home you can actually use. You do not need special design experience or a specific app.

What is Canva? Canva is an easy, browser-based design tool for making presentations, social posts, documents, simple logos, mood boards, and reusable templates. It is popular because you can drag and drop things instead of learning professional design software. There is a free version, and you can do everything you need for this exercise without becoming a designer.
Use the tool that already feels easiest

Canva is our easiest recommendation, but it is not a requirement. If you already use Google or Microsoft tools, stay there. The goal is to create one visual home for your brand—not learn another platform.

Canva
Best if you want easy drag-and-drop design, templates, mood boards, social graphics, and a visual Brand Kit.

You do not have to “be a designer.” Start simple. Your tool is just the place where you collect your choices and practice using them. Your first version can change. The goal is enough consistency that your brand starts to feel recognizable.
Before you open a design tool: have your 2–3 brand feelings, rough color direction, font direction, logo if you have one, and a small mood board. The tool should help you organize and create—not decide who you are.
1

Create one home for your brand

In Canva: use a Brand Kit if your plan includes it, or create a folder called My Brand and a multi-page design called My Brand Home. In Google Slides or PowerPoint: create one presentation called My Brand Home. Keep the same sections there.

2

Add your logo versions

Keep a primary logo, a simple version, an icon/mark if you have one, and light/dark versions. Avoid stretching, recoloring randomly, or putting the logo on backgrounds where it disappears.

Important: If you plan to trademark a logo, do not rely on a stock template or stock graphic as the final logo. Use original artwork or work with a designer for the final mark.
3

Save your colors with their exact codes

Use HEX codes for digital work. Label what each color is for: main, background, text, accent, highlight. This prevents “close enough” colors from creeping in.

4

Choose your fonts and give them jobs

Do not collect six “favorite” fonts. Decide which one is for headlines and which one is for readable body copy.

5

Add image examples, not just rules

Save 6–12 photos or visual examples that show the lighting, people, composition, texture, and energy you want. Add a few examples of what you do NOT want too.

6

Create a few starter templates

Start with what you will actually use: a social post, a presentation/page, a quote or teaching graphic, and one simple promotional or lead-magnet layout. If you only need two or three, that is enough.

7

Save a mini brand rules page

Include: your brand feelings, color use, font jobs, image style, logo rules, voice reminders, and a few “do / don’t” examples.

The “remove the logo” test
Open one of your designs and imagine the logo is gone. Does it still feel like your brand because of the color, typography, images, spacing, and voice? If yes, you are building an identity. If not, the logo is doing too much work.
